# Recommendations — Position, Counter-Proposal, and Next Steps

> Commercial guidance, not legal advice. Given the amounts involved and the
> proposed contract structure, a one-hour consultation with a lawyer before
> responding to the monthly-fee proposal is strongly recommended.

## A. Decide the position before the next contact

### 1. Do not sign the monthly-fee contract as proposed

It locks in a below-cost total, converts an existing debt into a cancellable
future service fee, leaves every meaningful promise (immediate payment, paid
before CNY) out of the document, and makes Clement a knowing party to a
contract whose stated purpose is to show the accountant something other than
the real arrangement. See `02-analysis.md` §5.

### 2. Fix a single number and stop re-formatting

Choose one basis and hold it. The defensible anchor is the **itemised bill
from Meeting 2**: it is derived from the discounted hourly rate Clement
already conceded, it matches the format other vendors (Zuh/Nabil) are paid
on, and it is the last version that was purely a *format* change rather than
a *price* change. Everything after it (cost-plus, monthly fee) was a further
price cut extracted without payment, and can legitimately be treated as
lapsed offers — they were each conditional on prompt payment that never came.

### 3. Set a floor and a walk-away

- **Floor:** never below cost. The opex is already disclosed, so state it
  plainly: "You've seen our costs. The monthly-fee total is below them. I
  can't fund SmartSpace's renovation at a loss."
- **Walk-away:** if no written agreement by a fixed date, the discounted
  pricing is withdrawn and the invoice reverts toward standard vendor pricing
  — the discounts were given in exchange for prompt payment, which did not
  happen.

## B. The counter-proposal

SmartSpace's one legitimate need in all of this is **cash-flow/budget
spreading across 2025–2026**. That can be met honestly, without a fake
service contract:

**Instalment payment agreement** for the 2025 renovation works:

1. A short agreement (or even a signed quotation + payment schedule) stating:
   Autolifetech completed renovation works in 2025; the total due is **[the
   itemised-bill amount]**; SmartSpace will pay it in instalments.
2. Schedule mirroring what Yvonne herself proposed — an upfront tranche now,
   the balance by a fixed calendar date **before Chinese New Year 2026**
   (write the actual date, e.g. "on or before 13 Feb 2026", not "before CNY").
3. Protective terms:
   - Late-payment interest or an agreed late fee after each due date.
   - Acceleration: if an instalment is missed, the full balance becomes due.
   - The discount is expressly conditional on the schedule being kept;
     otherwise the amount reverts to the itemised total (or standard pricing).
4. This gives Yvonne the budget split she wants, gives Aiveen a document that
   is actually true, and gives Autolifetech an enforceable debt instead of a
   cancellable service fee.

If Yvonne insists the paperwork must say "monthly fee": that is the point to
politely decline and hold — a request that the paperwork not match reality is
a risk transferred entirely onto Autolifetech.

## C. Change the process, not just the terms

1. **Move the negotiation to writing.** After every meeting (and starting
   with the next contact), send a same-day email: "Confirming what we
   discussed and agreed today: …". Silence in response to a clear written
   summary is far better evidence than a rushed verbal meeting. If Yvonne
   only ever decides verbally and reverses in the next meeting, email is the
   only way to stop the reset loop.
2. **Get a written acknowledgment of the debt.** Even one line from Yvonne in
   email/WhatsApp — "yes, we owe Autolifetech [amount] for the 2025 reno,
   we're working out the payment format" — transforms the legal position.
   A natural way to ask: "Before we finalise format, can we confirm the
   amount so I can close my 2025 books?"
3. **Preserve evidence now.** Export and archive: the Google Sheet (with
   revision history), every version of the bill (hourly, itemised,
   categories, cost-plus, monthly proposal), the opex submission, and all
   chats/emails around the meetings and payment promises.
4. **Stop delivering new work product for free.** No more re-formatted bills,
   costings, or internal data until something is paid or signed. Each
   deliverable so far has been consumed and answered with a new demand.
5. **Deadline discipline.** One clear offer (Section B), one reasonable
   deadline, stated consequence (reversion of discounts / formal recovery
   steps). Then follow through — the pattern only works on a counterparty
   who never enforces a deadline.

## D. Escalation path if the counter-proposal is refused

In order, each step reversible before the next:

1. **Formal invoice + letter of demand** on the itemised amount, with a
   payment deadline (14–30 days).
2. **Mediation** — often effective where the parties have an ongoing
   relationship and neither wants court.
3. **Statutory/legal recovery** — small claims tribunal or civil claim
   depending on the amount and jurisdiction; a lawyer's letter alone
   frequently unlocks payment from clients who pay other vendors normally.
4. Throughout: keep tone professional and non-personal. The message that
   preserves the friendship best is a consistent one — "the work is done,
   other vendors are paid on itemised invoices, I've offered the instalment
   plan you asked for; I just need this closed."
